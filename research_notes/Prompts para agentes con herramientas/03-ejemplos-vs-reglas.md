# Few-shot examples vs. declarative rules in prompts

## Do models follow demonstrations more strongly than stated instructions when the two conflict?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is now directly measured, not just anecdotal. A 2026 controlled study across 13 frontier models found demonstrated (in-context) behavior overrides explicit user instructions in a large share of cases, with the override rate rising as more conflicting examples accumulate, and a practitioner case study shows the same effect inside a single agent session with a skill-file rule. Chain-of-thought reasoning reduces but does not eliminate the effect, and some models even "think" the correct rule while outputting the pattern anyway.

### Cited Findings
- Camassa & Shiller, "Do as I Say, Not as I Do: Instruction-Induction Conflict in LLMs" (arXiv:2605.20382, submitted May 2026, revised Sept 2026; accepted as a poster at the Sci-FM Workshop, COLM 2026) built conversations where an explicit user instruction conflicted with a pattern demonstrated across prior assistant turns, varying the number of conflicting demonstrations (N = 1 to 50) and testing 13 models (Claude Opus/Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Flash, GPT‑5.2, Llama 3.1/3.3, OLMo 3.1, Hermes‑4, Gemma‑3, Kimi‑K2, Qwen variants). Average instruction-following rates ranged from 1% to 99% across models and were largely uncorrelated with standard capability benchmarks. — [arXiv:2605.20382](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.20382v3)
- In that same study, models stayed with the instruction longer when it aligned with trained values than when it didn't (32% vs. 18% instruction-following in fixed-output conditions), and task-based (more open-ended) conditions were more robust than fixed-output conditions (43% vs. 27% average instruction-following). Follow-up analysis attributed this to "output diversity," not deeper task engagement. — [arXiv:2605.20382](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.20382v3)
- Chain-of-thought reasoning improved robustness to the induction pressure but did not eliminate it; some models showed a "dissociation between internal deliberation and output," reasoning their way to the correct instruction but still emitting pattern-following tokens. — [arXiv:2605.20382](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.20382v3)
- Independent of that paper, Wei et al., "Larger language models do in-context learning differently" (arXiv:2303.03846, March 2023; tested GPT-3, InstructGPT, Codex, PaLM, Flan-PaLM) found that overriding semantic priors with in-context exemplars carrying *flipped* labels is an emergent ability of model scale — small models ignore the flipped labels and fall back on pretrained priors, while large models follow the in-context pattern even when it contradicts what they "know." Instruction tuning increased reliance on prior/semantic knowledge more than it increased willingness to learn a pure input-label mapping from examples. — [arXiv:2303.03846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03846)
- A practitioner experiment (Khuyen Tran, Sept 2026) on a commit-message agent skill with an explicit single-line-output rule found that injecting conflicting multi-line examples into prior conversation turns caused the rule to fail completely by 6 prior turns (5/5 test requests violated it), versus zero violations with clean history of any length; when the bad examples were followed by an explicit user correction, failure was merely delayed (to 15+ turns), not prevented. — [codecut.ai](https://codecut.ai/agent-skills-bad-session-history/)

### Inferences
- The "which wins" question does not have a universal answer — it is model-dependent and "largely uncorrelated with standard capability benchmarks" per Camassa & Shiller — but the direction of the effect (examples can and often do out-compete written instructions, and the effect grows with more/repeated examples) is now evidenced by a dedicated study, not merely folklore.
- This supports, rather than contradicts, the user's field observation: editing rules while leaving ~40 worked examples untouched is exactly the "fixed-output," repeated-pattern condition under which Camassa & Shiller found instruction-following drops hardest (27% average vs. 43% for diverse/task-based conditions), and it mirrors the codecut.ai mechanism where repeated conflicting demonstrations in context silently out-weigh a stated rule.

### Gaps
- Camassa & Shiller's setup is multi-turn *conversation history* conflict (instruction vs. prior assistant turns), not single-turn *system-prompt* conflict (instruction vs. worked examples embedded in the same system prompt). These are closely analogous but not identical experimental conditions; no paper found tests the exact "40 examples baked into one static system prompt vs. a numbered rule in the same prompt" configuration.
- No peer-reviewed, large-N study was found that isolates system-prompt-embedded examples (as opposed to conversational/session-history examples) as the conflicting signal.

## What does in-context learning literature say examples actually teach — the task, the label mapping, or mainly format/distribution?

### Takeaway
The seminal 2022 claim was that demonstrations mainly teach format, label space, and input distribution — not the correct input-label mapping — but this was directly disputed within the same year by a more fine-grained reanalysis, and the Wei et al. 2023 scaling study shows the answer flips with model size: small models learn mostly format/prior-retrieval, while large, instruction-tuned models can and do learn the input-label mapping itself when forced to (e.g., flipped or semantically-unrelated labels).

### Cited Findings
- Min et al., "Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations: What Makes In-Context Learning Work?" (EMNLP 2022; arXiv:2202.12837) found that randomly replacing the ground-truth labels in demonstrations barely hurt downstream accuracy across 12 models (including GPT-3), on a range of classification and multi-choice tasks. The paper attributes most of the benefit of demonstrations to exposure to the label space, the distribution of the input text, and the overall format of the sequence, rather than to correct input-label correspondence. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.759/), [arXiv:2202.12837](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12837)
- Kim et al., "Ground-Truth Labels Matter: A Deeper Look into Input-Label Demonstrations" (EMNLP 2022; arXiv:2205.12685) directly challenges Min et al., introducing new metrics (Ground-truth Label Effect Ratio, demo-gain, label sensitivity) and finding that correct input-label mapping has a far greater effect on in-context learning performance than Min et al. reported — explicitly stated as overturning the "ground-truth labels barely matter" claim under different measurement conditions. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.155/), [arXiv:2205.12685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.12685)
- Wei et al. (arXiv:2303.03846, 2023) reconciles the dispute by scale: small models ignore flipped labels and semantically-unrelated labels (consistent with "examples mostly teach format," as in Min et al.), while large models increasingly override their semantic priors to follow the in-context label mapping — i.e., for large models, examples *can* teach the actual task/mapping, not just format. — [arXiv:2303.03846](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03846)

### Inferences
- The disagreement between Min et al. and Kim et al., resolved partly by Wei et al., implies that "examples mainly teach format" is not a fixed truth about in-context learning — it is a property that depends on model scale/capability. For large, modern instruction-tuned models (the kind used in production agent system prompts), it is unsafe to assume examples are "just teaching format/tone" — they can and do teach (or override) the actual decision rule.
- For the user's system prompt: the fact that editing examples changed behavior while editing rules did not is consistent with the model having learned the actual behavior mapping from the ~40 examples (Kim et al./Wei et al. regime) rather than treating them as inert formatting illustrations of an already-dominant rule (the pure Min et al. reading).

### Gaps
- None of these papers studied natural-language, Spanish-language, multi-rule system prompts of the kind the user maintains; all are classification/multi-choice ICL benchmarks. Generalization from classification ICL to "does my 40-example conversational system prompt behave the same way" is an inference, not a directly tested claim.

## Example count and selection: how many help, when do they stop helping, and does a single unrepresentative example skew behavior?

### Takeaway
Provider guidance converges on roughly 3–5 well-chosen, diverse examples as the sweet spot; more examples add diminishing or negative returns (overfitting to the examples' incidental patterns), and a single skewed example reliably distorts predictions through majority-label and recency biases documented since Zhao et al. 2021.

### Cited Findings
- Zhao et al., "Calibrate Before Use: Improving Few-Shot Performance of Language Models" (ICML 2021; arXiv:2102.09690, tested on GPT-3-class models) identified three bias sources that destabilize few-shot predictions: majority label bias (an imbalanced set of examples biases the model toward the over-represented label), recency bias (labels nearer the end of the prompt disproportionately influence the next prediction, sometimes overriding majority bias), and common token bias (preference for pretraining-frequent tokens regardless of task fit). They propose "contextual calibration" against a content-free input to correct for these. — [ICML PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/zhao21c/zhao21c.pdf)
- Anthropic's current prompt-engineering guidance (platform.claude.com, "multishot prompting" / "claude-prompting-best-practices," applies to current Claude model family) recommends including 3–5 examples for best results, and explicitly instructs making examples "diverse: cover edge cases and vary enough that Claude doesn't pick up unintended patterns" — i.e., the provider explicitly names the "model learns an unintended incidental pattern from narrow examples" failure mode. — [Claude Platform Docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- Google's few-shot guidance (via Vertex AI / Gemini documentation, summarized by secondary sources) similarly recommends 3–5 examples, found that even 2–3 examples cut format errors by more than 50% versus zero-shot, but warns that too many examples can cause the model to overfit to the examples themselves, and recommends mixing the order/classes of examples rather than grouping them, to avoid recency/majority-style effects analogous to Zhao et al.'s findings. — [Google Cloud Vertex AI docs via secondary summary](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/learn/prompts/few-shot-examples)
- General few-shot prompting guidance (OpenAI-ecosystem summaries) converges on 2–10 examples depending on task complexity, with "at least one example per class" as a floor for multi-category tasks. — [PromptHub few-shot guide](https://www.prompthub.us/blog/the-few-shot-prompting-guide)

### Inferences
- "More examples = more coverage" is not supported past roughly 5 well-curated examples per sub-behavior; a system prompt with ~40 examples is almost certainly well past the point where each additional example is doing clean, additive work, and well into the regime where incidental shared surface features across examples (phrasing, ordering, which label appears last) start to dominate what the model actually learns — exactly Zhao et al.'s majority/recency mechanism, just at prompt-authoring time rather than at inference time.
- A large batch of examples authored at different times (an "August snapshot," per the user's own repo history) is structurally the same failure mode Zhao et al. describe for label imbalance/order: whichever behavior is over-represented or appears most recently in the example block has disproportionate pull on new cases, independent of what the numbered rules say.

### Gaps
- Zhao et al. and the provider guidance above are about in-context examples for single-turn classification/generation prompts, not specifically about a persistent system prompt with dozens of multi-turn conversational examples. No source quantifies "how many worked conversation examples is too many" for an agent system prompt specifically — this is an extrapolation from the classification-ICL literature and provider heuristics, not a directly measured number.

## Is there evidence that a stale or wrong example actively causes wrong behavior even when a correct rule is present elsewhere in the same prompt?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is the most directly on-point evidence for the user's situation: a 2026 practitioner study specifically tested "rule present, bad examples present" and found the examples won; a separate 2026 practitioner piece names the specific mechanism ("example drift") by which a once-correct example silently stops matching the rule it was meant to illustrate, including the sub-case where the example's incidental surface pattern gets learned instead of the intended rule.

### Cited Findings
- Khuyen Tran's commit-message-skill experiment (Sept 2026) kept a one-line-output rule constant in the skill file and only varied the conversation history: "clean" history (examples obeying the rule) produced zero violations at any history length tested; "conflict" history (examples violating the rule) produced complete rule failure by just 6 prior turns across all 5 test requests; adding an explicit user correction after the bad examples only delayed failure to 15+ turns, it did not prevent it. The author's stated conclusion: "Repeated bad examples can override the rules in a skill file." — [codecut.ai](https://codecut.ai/agent-skills-bad-session-history/)
- QA Skills' "Few-Shot Example Drift" piece (2026) names four decay mechanisms for a worked example that was correct when written: content drift (the example text is edited and no longer expresses the original rule), environment drift (the model, system instructions, tool catalog, or parser changes around a static example), traffic drift (real user inputs move away from what the example covers), and interpretation drift (a newer/different model infers a different general rule from the same demonstration than the one intended). It stresses that "examples radiate beyond the exact input" — a narrow example meant to teach one specific behavior can be generalized by the model into a much broader behavior change than intended. — [qaskills.sh](https://qaskills.sh/blog/prompt-testing-few-shot-example-drift)
- The same source recommends concrete maintenance practice directly applicable to a rules+examples prompt: write an explicit "intent card" per example (what it teaches, which input classes it covers, what it should *not* generalize to), run ablations (remove one example at a time and re-test) to find out what each example is actually doing, and sample production traffic against the example set to catch "traffic drift" before it causes silent misbehavior. — [qaskills.sh](https://qaskills.sh/blog/prompt-testing-few-shot-example-drift)

### Inferences
- Both sources are practitioner/blog-level evidence (not peer-reviewed), but they are directly on the user's exact question (rule present + bad/stale example present → which wins) in a way the academic ICL literature is not. Treat the *direction* of the finding (stale/conflicting examples can override a co-present rule) as well-supported by both an academic adjacent study (Camassa & Shiller) and these practitioner tests; treat the specific numbers (6 turns, 15 turns) as anecdotal to one narrow setup, not general thresholds.
- "Interpretation drift" and "examples radiate beyond the exact input" directly describes a plausible mechanism for the user's "editing examples changed behavior, editing rules didn't" observation: if even one of the ~40 examples is stale relative to current rules, the model may be generalizing a broader, wrong pattern from it that a correct rule elsewhere cannot override, and fixing that one example (removing/correcting the bad demonstration) would have an outsized, direct effect — matching what the user saw.

### Gaps
- No controlled, large-N academic study was found that isolates "one wrong example among many correct ones, rule stated separately" as its own variable (as opposed to "all examples wrong" as in the codecut.ai test, or "N accumulating conflicting examples" as in Camassa & Shiller). The user's specific scenario (mostly-correct example bank with a few stale ones) sits between these tested conditions and is inferred, not directly measured.

## Provider guidance on examples in agent system prompts: count, formatting, and keeping examples in sync with rules

### Takeaway
Anthropic's current guidance explicitly separates "clear and direct instructions" from "examples" as two distinct levers, recommends structuring both with XML tags so the model can tell them apart, caps examples at 3–5 for best results, and explicitly warns against narrow/non-diverse examples because the model will "pick up unintended patterns" from them — i.e., the provider's own documentation already names the failure mode the user observed as a known risk, not an edge case.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic's prompting guide states: "Examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer Claude's output format, tone, and structure. A few well-crafted examples (known as few-shot or multishot prompting) improve accuracy and consistency," and instructs that examples should be relevant (mirror the real use case), diverse (cover edge cases and vary enough that the model doesn't pick up unintended patterns), and structured (wrapped in `<example>`/`<examples>` tags so they're distinguishable from instructions). It recommends 3–5 examples for best results. — [Claude Platform Docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- The same guide separately recommends wrapping different prompt content types (instructions, context, examples, input) in distinct, consistently-named XML tags specifically "to reduce misinterpretation" when a prompt mixes instructions and examples — implying that unstructured mixing of rules and examples (plain numbered rules + plain worked-example transcripts, with no tag separation) is itself a known risk factor for the model blending or confusing the two. — [Claude Platform Docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- Anthropic's multi-agent engineering writeup describes their own prompting philosophy as "instilling good heuristics rather than rigid rules" learned from observing skilled humans, with explicit guardrails to prevent unintended behaviors — this post does not discuss example-count or example/rule conflict directly, so it should not be read as guidance on that specific question. — [Anthropic engineering blog](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
- OpenAI's prompting guide (developers.openai.com) recommends combining few-shot examples into a "concise YAML-style or bulleted block so your team can scan and update them," and separating tone/role guidance (system message) from task-specific details and examples (user message), but does not give an explicit example count or address what happens when examples and instructions conflict. — [OpenAI prompting guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompting)

### Inferences
- The user's prompt structure — numbered rules plus ~40 worked examples, in Spanish, presumably without strict structural separation and without a maintained "what does each example teach" index — is close to what Anthropic's own documentation flags as risky on two independent axes: too many examples past the 3–5 recommendation, and (if unstructured) a format where the model may not cleanly distinguish instruction content from example content.
- No provider source found gives an authoritative answer for "we have far more than 5 examples already, how do we keep them from drifting" beyond the practitioner "intent card" / ablation practice found in the "Gaps" item below — this is a maintenance gap in official guidance, not just in the user's prompt.

### Gaps
- No official Anthropic, OpenAI, or Google documentation was found addressing explicitly what to do for a mature prompt that already has 10x+ the recommended example count (i.e., migration/remediation guidance, as opposed to greenfield guidance of "use 3–5"). The practitioner material (qaskills.sh) is the closest available source for that remediation question, but it is not official provider guidance.

## Guidance on examples in tool-using agents specifically (demonstrating tool calls vs. describing when to call)

### Takeaway
Available official guidance for tool-using agents favors precise, explicit *instructions* about when and how to act (including directive imperative phrasing) over relying on demonstrated tool-call transcripts, and documents that insufficiently explicit instructions cause the model to default to suggesting rather than acting — but no provider source found gives explicit comparative guidance on "demonstrate a tool call" vs. "describe the triggering condition in prose" as competing techniques.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic's tool-use guidance states that current Claude models "are trained for precise instruction following and benefit from explicit direction to use specific tools," and that vague phrasing ("can you suggest some changes") leads the model to merely suggest rather than act, whereas direct imperative phrasing ("change this function to improve its performance") reliably triggers the tool-using/editing behavior. This is instruction-phrasing guidance, not example-based. — [Claude Platform Docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)
- The same documentation set shows a worked system-prompt snippet for parallel tool-call behavior (`<use_parallel_tool_calls>` block) as a rule written in prose/pseudo-instruction form describing the desired policy (when to parallelize vs. sequence calls), not as a transcript of example tool calls — consistent with the broader pattern that Anthropic's own example tool-use prompts tend to state the policy directly rather than rely on the model inferring it from a demonstrated call sequence. — [Claude Platform Docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)

### Inferences
- The absence of explicit "demonstrate N tool calls" guidance from either Anthropic or OpenAI's current docs, combined with their emphasis on explicit imperative instruction phrasing for triggering tool use, suggests the safer default for tool-triggering logic specifically is a clearly stated rule/condition rather than a worked example a model must generalize from — tool-call correctness (right tool, right arguments, right timing) is more brittle to incidental example surface-pattern-learning (Min et al./Zhao et al. mechanisms) than free-text style is.

### Gaps
- No primary source (Anthropic, OpenAI, or Google) was found making a direct comparative claim about demonstrating tool calls vs. describing trigger conditions in prose; this section's inference is extrapolated from general instruction-vs-example findings above plus Anthropic's general tool-triggering guidance, not from guidance specific to the trade-off asked about. This is the weakest-evidenced key question in this note.
